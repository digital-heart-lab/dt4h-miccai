Talk Title: 
From Spins to Images to Digital Twins and Back Again
Talk Abstract: 
This lecture will reflect on the evolution of cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging from accelerated image reconstruction toward physics-informed digital twinning, highlighting how recent advances in artificial intelligence, computational modeling, and data-driven inference are reshaping the field. Beginning with the transition from supervised to self-supervised and generalizable reconstruction methods, the lecture will discuss how modern learning-based approaches can recover high-quality images from highly undersampled data while reducing dependence on fully sampled references. The presentation will further examine the growing role of biophysical modeling and synthetic data generation in overcoming one of the central challenges in medical AI: the lack of reliable ground truth. By integrating computational physiology, virtual MRI scanners, and controllable image synthesis, emerging frameworks now enable the generation of realistic paired datasets for training, validation, and uncertainty quantification of AI/ML methods in cardiovascular imaging. Examples including FlowMRI-Net, synthetic 4D/5D flow MRI, and physics-informed late gadolinium enhancement synthesis will illustrate how these tools are advancing robust image analysis and inference. Finally, the lecture will place these developments into the broader context of personalized cardiovascular medicine through the concept of digital twins. The SwissHeart initiative will serve as an example of how multimodal imaging, physiological measurements, biomarkers, and computational models can be integrated to create population-scale digital twins capable of characterizing healthy variation, tracking disease progression, and ultimately informing individualized prediction and intervention. The keynote will conclude with a perspective on future opportunities and challenges for uncertainty-aware, interpretable, and clinically deployable AI in cardiovascular imaging.
Short Bio
Sebastian Kozerke, PhD, is Professor of Biomedical Imaging at ETH Zurich and the University of Zurich, where he leads research in cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging, computational modeling, and AI-driven biomedical imaging. His work spans from MR physics, accelerated image reconstruction, flow imaging and tissue characterization to physics-informed digital twinning for personalized cardiovascular medicine. After studying electrical and biomedical engineering, he obtained his PhD and Venia legendi from ETH Zurich and subsequently held research and academic appointments at King's College London before returning to Zurich. He is also co-founder of GyroTools, supporting the translation of MRI research into clinical and research applications. Sebastian Kozerke has made internationally recognized contributions to cardiovascular MRI, including accelerated and self-supervised image reconstruction, quantitative flow imaging, MR spectroscopy, and the integration of biophysical modeling with machine learning. His current research focuses on uncertainty-aware AI, synthetic imaging, and the creation of personalized cardiovascular digital twins to improve disease prediction and individualized therapy. He is the author of more than 340 peer-reviewed journal articles and 8 patents.

Talk Title:
Towards Digital Twins in Neurodegeneration: From Brain Dynamics to Personalized Medicine
Talk Abstract:
Computational models are increasingly becoming mechanistic frameworks that link biological processes with clinical observations. In this talk, I will discuss how mechanistic whole-brain computational models can infer latent physiological alterations directly from electrophysiological recordings, providing quantitative biomarkers inaccessible to conventional analyses. Drawing on recent work, I will illustrate how personalized models might capture disease-specific alterations, for example in terms of neural excitability, conduction delays, or the effect of drugs, offering mechanistic explanations for clinical heterogeneity and disease progression. I will give examples for multiple diseases. Finally, I will discuss the emerging concept of the digital twin: individualized computational representations of patients that integrate multimodal data to improve prediction of clinically relevant features and ultimately enable precision neurology.
Short Bio:
Pierpaolo Sorrentino, MD, PhD, is a Principal Investigator at the Institut de Neurosciences des Systèmes (Inserm and Aix-Marseille University, France). He is a board-certified neurologist and holds a Ph.D. in Engineering. He subsequently worked as a postdoctoral researcher within the Human Brain Project, where he developed computational approaches to investigate large-scale brain dynamics, with a focus on neurodegenerative diseases. His research has made extensive use of magnetoencephalography (MEG) to characterize functional brain networks and identify electrophysiological biomarkers, specifically in the context of critical dynamics. More recently, his group has focused on mechanistic whole-brain modeling, integrating electrophysiology, neuroimaging, and network science to infer latent physiological mechanisms underlying neurological diseases. His work has contributed to personalized computational models of Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and other brain disorders, with the long-term goal of developing clinically actionable digital twins for diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment optimization. He has authored 200 peer-reviewed scientific publications.
